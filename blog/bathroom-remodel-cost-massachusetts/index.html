How Much Does a Bathroom Remodel Cost in Massachusetts? (2026 North Shore Guide)

Typical 2026 price ranges on the North Shore, where the money goes, and the hidden items that blow a bathroom budget.

By Luiz DeFaria, Owner · DeFaria Construction · Updated October 2026

By Luiz DeFaria, Owner of DeFaria Construction · BBB Accredited A+ · Updated October 2026

Here is the uncomfortable truth about bathroom remodel cost: the number on the first estimate is rarely the number on the final invoice. Not because the contractor lied, but because a bathroom is the room where the most expensive problems hide behind the tile, under the tub and inside the walls.

If you own one of the older homes that fill the North Shore, a colonial in Salem, a cape in Beverly, a ranch in Peabody, you know the feeling. You want a new bathroom. You also want to know what it will really cost before anyone swings a hammer.

Here are the typical 2026 price ranges in Essex and Middlesex County, where the money goes, what each type of bathroom tends to cost, and how to spot the estimate that is hiding something. It is a long guide on purpose. Use the headings to jump to the part that matches your project.

How much does a bathroom remodel cost on the North Shore?

For a typical full bathroom in a single-family home or condo, most 2026 projects fall into one of three ranges:

  • Cosmetic refresh: roughly $10,000 to $20,000. New vanity, toilet, faucet, lighting, mirror and paint, maybe new flooring. The tub or shower stays. Plumbing does not move.
  • Mid-range full remodel: roughly $20,000 to $45,000. Everything comes out. New tile, new tub or a tub-to-shower conversion, vanity, fixtures, lighting and ventilation, with plumbing in roughly the same place. This is the most common project we scope.
  • Full gut or layout change: roughly $45,000 to $80,000 and up. Walls opened to the studs, plumbing relocated, the room reconfigured, curbless shower, heated floors, custom vanity.

These are typical ranges, not promises. Labor and permitting around greater Boston run above the national numbers in online calculators, so a "$12,000 bathroom" from a national website rarely survives a local bid.

So when someone asks about the average bathroom remodel cost, or the cost to redo a bathroom from scratch, the honest answer is a range. Bathroom renovation cost depends on what is already there. The same room can land at $25,000 or $50,000, and the difference is the decisions you make and what the walls are hiding.

Half bath and powder room remodel cost

A half bath, sometimes called a powder room, has a toilet and a sink and nothing else. Because there is no tub or shower, there is no wet-area waterproofing, which is one of the most labor-intensive parts of any bathroom. That is why a half bath typically runs from roughly $6,000 to $15,000 on the North Shore.

At the low end, you are replacing the toilet, vanity or pedestal sink, faucet, mirror, light and floor, then painting. At the high end, you are adding wainscoting or wall tile, a custom or furniture-style vanity, a wall-mounted faucet or a new location for the toilet.

Powder rooms are also where homeowners can afford to be bold. The room is small, so a dramatic wallpaper, a stone vessel sink or a statement light fixture costs far less than it would in a full bath. Many colonials in Danvers, Andover and Lynnfield have a first-floor half bath that has not been touched in decades, and updating it is one of the most visible improvements you can make for guests.

What surprises people: older half baths tucked under a staircase often have tight clearances and odd ceiling angles. Fitting a modern toilet and vanity into that footprint sometimes takes custom carpentry, which belongs in the estimate from the start.

Full hall bathroom remodel cost

The full hall bath is the shared bathroom upstairs in most colonials and capes: tub with shower, toilet, single vanity, usually somewhere between five by seven and five by nine feet. It is also the room that gets the most daily use and the most abuse from water.

A cosmetic refresh of a hall bath typically runs from roughly $10,000 to $20,000. A mid-range remodel where everything comes out and goes back in roughly the same place typically runs from roughly $20,000 to $40,000.

The money in a hall bath goes into the tub area. Removing an old cast iron tub, checking the subfloor, installing a new tub or shower pan, waterproofing the walls and setting tile takes more days than every other part of the room combined. If the hall bath is the only full bathroom in the house, the schedule matters as much as the budget, because your family loses the room for the duration. A good contractor will plan the work to keep that window as short as possible and tell you upfront how many days you will be without a shower.

Primary bathroom remodel cost

A primary bathroom, the one attached to the main bedroom, is where most of the high-end projects live. Typical 2026 ranges on the North Shore start around $35,000 and often reach $80,000 or more for a full gut with layout changes.

What makes primary baths more expensive is not just size. It is what homeowners want in them: a large tiled walk-in shower with a bench and niches, a double vanity with a stone top, a freestanding tub, heated floors, a separate toilet room, upgraded lighting and better ventilation. Each of those is reasonable on its own. Together they add up quickly.

Primary bathroom projects are also where layout changes happen. Swapping the tub and shower locations, removing a linen closet to gain space, or borrowing a few feet from the bedroom all involve moving plumbing and framing, which is where costs climb. If you are planning a primary suite in a larger home, for example the bathroom remodeling projects we scope in North Andover and the inland towns, decide early which features matter most so the budget goes there first.

Basement bathroom cost

Adding a bathroom to a finished or partially finished basement is a different kind of project, and it usually costs more than people expect. Typical 2026 ranges run from roughly $25,000 to $55,000.

The reason is drainage. In many North Shore homes, the main sewer line leaves the house above the basement floor, which means a basement toilet and shower cannot drain by gravity. The usual solutions are an ejector pump system or an above-floor macerating system, and both add cost. If the drain line is below the slab, the concrete must be cut and trenched to tie in, which is messy but often the more durable choice.

Basements also bring moisture. A basement bathroom needs strong exhaust ventilation, moisture-tolerant materials and, in many cases, attention to the foundation walls before any finishes go up. Skipping that step is how a new basement bath ends up smelling like a basement bath within a year.

A basement bathroom can be worth it, especially if the basement is used as a guest suite, playroom or home office. Just price it as its own project, not as a smaller version of an upstairs remodel.

Bathroom remodel cost per square foot

Homeowners often ask for a price per square foot, and it can be a useful sanity check as long as you know its limits. Bathrooms are small rooms with a lot of fixed costs: one toilet, one vanity, one shower valve, one permit, one exhaust fan. Those costs stay roughly the same whether the room is 40 square feet or 60.

That is why bathrooms have a high cost per square foot compared with almost any other room in the house. As a typical range on the North Shore:

  • Small hall baths (about 35 to 50 square feet), mid-range remodel: often roughly $450 to $900 per square foot.
  • Larger primary baths (about 80 to 150 square feet): often roughly $300 to $650 per square foot, because the fixed costs are spread over more space.
  • High-end or full layout changes: can go well above these ranges.

Use per-square-foot numbers to check that an estimate is in the right neighborhood, not to build a budget. Two bathrooms of the same size can differ by tens of thousands of dollars depending on what is behind the walls and what you choose to put in them.

Where the money goes

In a mid-range bathroom, the real cost is the work you never see once the room is finished.

  • Labor and installation. Demolition, framing repairs, waterproofing, tile setting and finish carpentry. Tile is slow, skilled work, and it is where quality shows for the next twenty years.
  • Waterproofing and tile. The shower system matters more than the tile you pick. Proper waterproofing costs more upfront and prevents the leak that rots a subfloor five years later.
  • Plumbing. New valves, drains and supply lines. Cost climbs quickly if anything moves.
  • Vanity. The cost to install a new bathroom vanity typically runs from about $1,500 for a simple stock unit to $5,000 or more for a custom piece with a stone top.
  • Tub or shower. Bathtub replacement cost usually falls between $3,500 and $10,000 installed, depending on the tub, the surround and what the old one was hiding.
  • Electrical and ventilation. GFCI outlets, layered lighting and a properly sized exhaust fan.

If your goal is to make the room feel bigger, our guide to small bathroom remodel ideas for North Shore homes covers the design side. This article is about the money.

The items that blow budgets in old North Shore homes

Every item in the next five sections is common in the housing stock between Lynn and Newburyport, and every one can be anticipated by a contractor who has opened enough of these walls. None of them should be a surprise on the invoice. They should be a line on the estimate, even if that line is an allowance.

Rotten subfloor under the tub

Decades of small leaks around an old tub or toilet often rot the subfloor, sometimes the joists too. You cannot see it until the tub comes out. A good estimate includes an allowance for it or states what the repair costs if it is found. Coastal homes see this often, so if you are planning bathroom remodeling in Marblehead or Swampscott, expect someone to check under that tub.

Signs you may have a problem before demolition: a soft spot near the toilet, a toilet that rocks, tile that cracks along the same line, or a stain on the ceiling below. Any of these is worth mentioning when you ask for an estimate, so the contractor can plan for it instead of reacting to it.

Old plumbing that must be replaced

Galvanized supply lines, cast iron drains and outdated shower valves are everywhere in pre-1960 homes. Once the walls are open, connecting new fixtures to old pipes rarely makes sense. That replacement belongs in the plan from day one.

Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside, which is why older homes often have weak water pressure in the shower. Old shower valves may also lack the pressure-balancing protection modern valves provide against sudden temperature swings. Replacing them while the wall is open costs a fraction of what it would cost to open the wall again later.

Ventilation that never existed

Many older bathrooms have no exhaust fan, or one that vents into the attic. A proper duct to the exterior is not glamorous, but it keeps your new tile from growing mildew.

In a New England winter, warm, wet bathroom air that ends up in a cold attic condenses on the roof sheathing. Over time that can mean mold in the attic, not just in the bathroom. Venting the fan to the outside is one of the cheapest insurance policies in the whole project.

Wiring brought up to code

Opening the walls usually means updating the circuit and adding GFCI protection. In homes we see for bathroom remodeling in Salem and Beverly, outdated wiring is one of the most common surprises when the plaster comes down.

Older homes may have ungrounded circuits, wiring shared with other rooms, or a single overhead light and no outlet near the sink at all. Modern bathrooms need dedicated circuits for outlets, proper lighting over the vanity and in the shower, and power for the exhaust fan and any heated floor. Plan for it so it is in the price, not a change order.

Permits in Massachusetts

Most bathroom remodels that touch plumbing, electrical or framing require permits in Massachusetts, often a building permit plus plumbing and electrical permits. Skipping them does not save money. It moves the cost to the day you sell the house.

Permits also bring inspections, which protect you. A plumbing inspector checks the rough-in before the walls close. An electrical inspector checks the wiring. That is a second set of eyes on the work you will never see again once the tile goes up. Each town handles permits through its own building department, and timing varies, so a contractor who works in your town regularly will know how long to plan for.

Tub-to-shower conversion: what it really costs

Turning an old tub into a walk-in shower is one of the most requested projects we price. It typically runs from roughly $9,000 to $20,000 in our area. What pushes it higher: a curbless entry that requires lowering the floor, moving the valve or drain, frameless glass, and floor-to-ceiling tile with built-in niches.

If it is the only bathroom in the house, think twice before removing the last tub, especially with young kids. In a second bathroom or primary suite, the conversion is almost always worth it. It is the question homeowners bring most often when they ask about bathroom remodeling in Andover.

Walk-in shower cost

Beyond the conversion, many homeowners want a true walk-in shower built from scratch, especially in a primary bathroom. A fully tiled walk-in shower typically runs from roughly $12,000 to $30,000 on the North Shore, depending on size, tile, glass and features.

What goes into that number:

  • The shower pan or base. A prefabricated base costs less. A custom tiled floor with a linear drain costs more and looks seamless.
  • Waterproofing. A membrane system behind and under the tile, which we cover in its own section below.
  • The valve and trim. A single shower head with a standard valve is the baseline. A thermostatic valve, handheld shower on a slide bar and a separate rain head each add cost.
  • Glass. A framed door is the economical choice. Frameless or partially frameless glass looks cleaner and typically costs more.
  • Built-ins. Recessed niches and a bench are popular and practical, and they add labor because every edge must be waterproofed and finished.

The most important decision is not the tile. It is the slope and drainage of the floor. A shower that drains poorly will always look like a bad shower, no matter how beautiful the finishes are.

Freestanding tub vs alcove tub

A freestanding tub is one of the most photographed features in bathroom design, and one of the most misunderstood in terms of cost.

An alcove tub sits between three walls with a tiled or paneled surround. It is efficient with space, doubles as a shower, and is the standard in hall baths. Replacing one typically falls inside the $3,500 to $10,000 bathtub replacement range.

A freestanding tub sits on its own, usually in a primary bathroom. The tub itself can range widely in price, but the hidden costs are what matter:

  • Floor-mounted filler. Many freestanding tubs need a faucet that rises from the floor, which means plumbing run through the floor and blocking for support.
  • Floor strength. Cast iron and stone tubs filled with water are heavy. In older homes, the joists may need reinforcement.
  • Space. A freestanding tub needs room to clean around and to step in and out comfortably.
  • Getting it upstairs. Narrow stairways in older colonials sometimes make delivery its own small project.

If you take baths regularly and have the room, a freestanding tub can be worth it. If the tub would mostly be decorative, many homeowners are happier putting that money into a larger shower.

Tile and waterproofing cost

Tile is the most visible finish in a bathroom, and waterproofing is the most important thing you will never see. They are priced together because they are installed together.

As a typical range, a fully tiled tub or shower surround runs roughly $4,000 to $10,000 installed on the North Shore, and tile floors add more depending on size and pattern. The tile itself is often the smaller part of that number. The labor of preparing the surface, waterproofing, setting and grouting is the larger part.

Factors that push tile cost up:

  • Large-format tile. It looks clean but needs very flat walls and floors, which in older homes often means extra prep.
  • Small mosaic or intricate patterns. Herringbone, hexagon and mosaic take longer to set.
  • Natural stone. Marble and similar stones look beautiful and need sealing and more careful installation.
  • Tile to the ceiling. More square footage, more labor, a more finished look.

On waterproofing, ask what system will be used behind the tile in wet areas. A modern sheet or liquid membrane system adds cost upfront and is far cheaper than repairing a leak that rots the framing years later. If an estimate does not mention waterproofing at all, ask why.

Vanity and countertop cost

The vanity is the piece of furniture in the bathroom, and it sets the tone for the room. The cost to install a new bathroom vanity typically runs from about $1,500 for a simple stock unit to $5,000 or more for a custom piece with a stone top.

Here is how the options usually break down:

  • Stock vanities. Ready-made units in standard sizes. The most affordable and the fastest to get.
  • Semi-custom vanities. More choices of size, finish and storage layout. A middle ground in price and lead time.
  • Custom vanities. Built to the exact dimensions of the room. Useful in older homes where the walls are not square or the space is unusual, and the most expensive option.
  • Floating vanities. Wall-mounted units that make a small bathroom feel larger and make the floor easier to clean. They need solid blocking in the wall.

For countertops, quartz is the most requested surface for its durability and low maintenance. Natural stone brings more character and needs periodic sealing. A double vanity in a primary bathroom adds a second sink, faucet and drain, which adds plumbing cost on top of the larger top. Before choosing a size, measure how much floor space you need to open drawers and doors comfortably, because the vanity that looks best in the showroom is not always the one that fits.

Heated floors and ventilation upgrades

Two upgrades that New England homeowners rarely regret are heated floors and a better exhaust fan.

Heated floors. Electric radiant mats under the tile typically add roughly $1,500 to $4,000 to a bathroom project, depending on the size of the heated area and the thermostat. They need a dedicated circuit and must be installed before the tile goes down, so the decision has to be made early. On a January morning in Gloucester or Burlington, homeowners consistently tell us it is the feature they use most.

Ventilation. A properly sized, quiet exhaust fan vented to the outside typically costs roughly $500 to $1,500 installed, more if a new duct has to be run through a finished ceiling. Options like a humidity-sensing switch or a timer help the fan run long enough to actually clear the moisture. A quiet fan matters more than it sounds: people turn off fans they find loud, and a fan that is off does nothing.

Accessibility and aging in place

More homeowners on the North Shore are remodeling with the next twenty years in mind, whether for themselves or for a parent who may move in. Aging-in-place features cost far less to build into a remodel than to add later.

Common accessibility features and what affects their cost:

  • Blocking for grab bars. Wood blocking inside the walls costs very little during a remodel and lets you add grab bars anywhere later. Even if you do not want grab bars today, ask for the blocking.
  • Grab bars. Modern grab bars come in finishes that match the rest of the fixtures and look like towel bars.
  • Curbless shower. A zero-threshold entry removes the step into the shower. It often requires lowering or reframing the floor, which can add roughly $3,000 to $8,000 or more depending on the structure.
  • Comfort-height toilet. A taller toilet that is easier to sit on and stand up from, with little or no added cost.
  • Wider doorway. Widening a bathroom door for a walker or wheelchair involves framing and trim work.
  • Handheld shower and bench. Simple, inexpensive and useful for everyone.
  • Lever handles and better lighting. Easier to use, easier to see.

Done well, an accessible bathroom does not look clinical. It looks like a well-designed modern bathroom that happens to be easier to use.

Condo bathroom remodel

Condo bathrooms are common across the North Shore, from converted buildings in Salem and Newburyport to newer complexes in the inland towns. The work is similar to a single-family home, but the rules are not.

What changes in a condo:

  • Board or management approval. Many associations require written approval, plans and a description of the work before anything starts.
  • Certificate of insurance. Most buildings require the contractor to provide proof of insurance naming the association.
  • Work hours. Noisy work is often limited to specific weekday hours, which can stretch the schedule.
  • Elevator and parking. Some buildings require elevator reservations and protection, and parking for a crew and a dumpster may be limited.
  • Shared plumbing. Drains and supply risers often serve units above and below. Shutting off water may require coordination with management, and moving fixtures may be restricted.
  • Sound and flooring rules. Some associations require sound-dampening underlayment under hard floors.

A typical condo bathroom remodel runs roughly $18,000 to $45,000 depending on scope. Read your association documents before you plan, and give your contractor a copy so the estimate reflects the building's rules.

How costs change across Essex and Middlesex towns

The North Shore does not have one housing stock. It has several, and the type of house affects the cost of a bathroom more than the zip code does.

Older coastal towns. In Salem, Marblehead, Swampscott, Gloucester and Newburyport, many homes are well over a century old. Expect plaster walls, older plumbing and framing that was built before modern standards. Bathrooms in these homes often sit in rooms that were not originally designed as bathrooms. That character is part of the appeal, and it is also why projects here tend to need more allowance for hidden conditions.

Mid-century neighborhoods. Capes, ranches and splits from the decades after the war are common in Peabody, Danvers, Beverly and Lynn. Bathrooms tend to be small and standardized, which makes them more predictable to remodel, though galvanized pipe and original tile over a mortar bed are common.

Newer suburbs. In parts of Andover, North Andover, Lynnfield, Winchester and Lexington, newer homes often have larger primary baths and modern plumbing. The cost there is driven less by surprises and more by finish level and features.

The practical takeaway: an experienced local contractor should adjust the allowance for hidden conditions based on the age and type of your house, not apply one formula to every town.

How long a bathroom remodel takes, phase by phase

A typical full bathroom remodel takes three to six weeks once work begins. Here is how that time usually breaks down for a mid-range remodel:

  1. Planning and ordering (before work starts). Selecting tile, vanity and fixtures and confirming they are in stock. Custom vanities and specialty glass can take several weeks, which is why materials should be ordered before demolition.
  2. Demolition (one to three days). Removing the old tub, tile, vanity and fixtures and protecting the rest of the house from dust.
  3. Repairs and framing (one to several days). Fixing any subfloor or framing problems, adding blocking and adjusting walls.
  4. Rough plumbing and electrical (several days). New valves, drains, supply lines, circuits, lighting and fan duct, followed by inspections.
  5. Walls, waterproofing and tile (one to two weeks). Cement board or panels, waterproofing membrane, then tile setting and grouting. This is the longest phase.
  6. Fixtures and finish (about one week). Vanity, toilet, faucet, shower trim, glass, lighting, accessories and paint.
  7. Final inspection and walkthrough. Checking every detail with you before the project closes.

Layout changes, custom glass and backordered materials are the most common causes of delay. A clear schedule in writing helps you plan around the days you lose the room.

How to save money without cutting the wrong corners

There are smart ways to lower the cost of a bathroom remodel, and there are ways that cost more later. Here is how to tell them apart.

Smart places to save:

  • Keep the layout. Leaving the toilet, tub and sink where they are is the single biggest way to control cost.
  • Use standard-size products. A standard tub, a stock or semi-custom vanity and readily available tile cost less and arrive faster.
  • Mix premium and affordable tile. Use a special tile on one accent wall or the shower floor and a simpler tile elsewhere.
  • Choose a framed or partially frameless shower door. It looks clean and costs less than full frameless glass.
  • Make decisions early. Changes after work begins are the most expensive way to change anything.

Where not to cut:

  • Waterproofing. It is invisible and it is the difference between a bathroom that lasts and one that rots.
  • Ventilation. A good fan vented outside protects every other finish in the room.
  • The shower valve. Cheap valves fail, and replacing one means opening the tiled wall.
  • Subfloor repairs. Tile over a soft floor cracks.
  • Licensed trades and permits. Saving here costs more when you sell, insure or need a repair.

DIY vs licensed contractor in Massachusetts

Some homeowners consider doing part of the work themselves. Painting, installing accessories and even demolition can be reasonable DIY tasks for a handy homeowner. The rest of a bathroom is a different story in Massachusetts.

In Massachusetts, plumbing work generally must be performed by a licensed plumber, and electrical work by a licensed electrician, with permits pulled through your town. Residential contractors doing home improvement work on owner-occupied homes are generally required to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) with the state, and structural work typically requires a Construction Supervisor License. The state also maintains a Guaranty Fund that can offer homeowners some protection when they hire a registered contractor.

Before you hire anyone, ask for their HIC registration number and license information and confirm them with the state. Ask for proof of insurance. A written contract with the scope, price and payment schedule is standard practice and protects both sides.

The real cost of DIY in a bathroom is not usually the materials. It is the mistake that shows up as a leak months later, after the tile is set and the walls are closed.

Does a bathroom remodel pay off when you sell?

A bathroom remodel is first an investment in how you live in the house. That said, bathrooms consistently rank among the rooms buyers notice most, and an outdated bathroom is one of the first things that makes a house feel like a project.

From a resale point of view, a few principles hold up well:

  • Fix the function first. A leaking shower, a soft floor or a missing exhaust fan will show up in a home inspection and give a buyer leverage to negotiate.
  • Match the house. A bathroom finished in line with the rest of the home tends to help a sale more than one that is far above or below it.
  • Choose timeless over trendy. Neutral tile and classic fixtures appeal to more buyers than a bold look that may date quickly.
  • Keep a tub somewhere. In a home with one full bath, many families with young children still want a tub.
  • Permits matter. Work done with permits and inspections is easier to document when the buyer's agent asks.

If you plan to sell soon, a well-done mid-range remodel usually makes more sense than a high-end one. If you plan to stay for years, build the bathroom you want to use every day.

How to read a bathroom remodel estimate

A one-line estimate that says "Bathroom remodel: $28,000" tells you nothing. A real estimate shows:

  1. Scope in writing. What comes out, what goes in, what stays.
  2. Materials or allowances. The specific tile, vanity and fixtures, or a dollar allowance for each.
  3. Plumbing and electrical scope. Replaced or reconnected, and whether anything moves.
  4. Contingency items. What happens if the subfloor is rotten or the wiring is outdated, and roughly what it costs.
  5. Permits. Who pulls them and whether fees are included.
  6. Schedule. How many weeks, which phases, and when you lose use of the room.

If two estimates are $10,000 apart, the gap is almost always in items 3 and 4. The cheaper one did not find a better price. It left something out.

Checklist before you ask for an estimate

You will get a faster, more accurate estimate if you prepare a few things before the first visit:

  • Your goals. What is not working today? Storage, lighting, a tub you never use, a shower that leaks, a room that feels cramped.
  • A budget range. Even a rough range helps a contractor suggest the right options instead of guessing.
  • Photos you like. A few saved images say more than a long description.
  • Must-haves and nice-to-haves. Separate what you need from what you would like if the budget allows.
  • Known problems. Soft spots, leaks, stains on the ceiling below, weak water pressure, a fan that does not work.
  • The age of the house. And any previous work you know about in the bathroom.
  • Your timeline. When you want work to start and any dates it must be finished by.
  • Condo documents. If you live in a condo, the association rules for renovations.

Questions to ask a bathroom contractor

Use these questions to compare contractors on more than price:

  1. Are you registered as a Home Improvement Contractor in Massachusetts, and can I see your license and insurance?
  2. Who pulls the permits, and are permit fees included in the estimate?
  3. What waterproofing system do you use in the shower?
  4. What is included as an allowance, and what happens if a selection costs more?
  5. How do you handle hidden conditions like a rotten subfloor or old plumbing?
  6. Who will be on site each day, and who is my point of contact?
  7. How will you protect the rest of my house from dust?
  8. How many weeks will the project take, and how long will I be without the bathroom?
  9. How will you keep me updated while the work is in progress?
  10. What does your warranty cover after the project is finished?

A contractor who answers these clearly and in writing is usually the one whose final invoice matches the estimate.

When to schedule your bathroom remodel

Bathrooms are interior projects, so they can be done any time of year in Massachusetts. Timing still matters, mostly because of demand.

Searches for bathroom remodel cost peak around March, and spring calendars fill up fast as homeowners plan projects before summer. If you want work done in the spring, starting the conversation in winter gives you time to compare estimates, make selections and order materials before demolition.

Winter can also be a good time to start the work itself. Interior projects are not affected by the weather, and planning ahead makes it easier to secure the start date you want. If you are remodeling before hosting a holiday or selling in the spring, work backward from that date and leave room for material lead times.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a bathroom remodel cost in Massachusetts in 2026?

Most full bathroom remodels in Essex and Middlesex County fall between $20,000 and $45,000 for a mid-range project. Cosmetic refreshes typically start around $10,000, and full gut remodels with layout changes can reach $80,000 or more. These are typical ranges; your actual number depends on scope, materials and the condition of the plumbing and subfloor.

What is the average bathroom remodel cost for an older North Shore home?

Older homes usually land in the middle to upper part of the range because of hidden conditions: rotten subfloor under the tub, outdated plumbing, missing ventilation and wiring that must be brought to code. A contractor experienced with older New England houses can anticipate most of these in the estimate.

How much does it cost to replace a bathtub?

Bathtub replacement typically costs between $3,500 and $10,000 installed in our area, depending on the tub, the surround or tile and any repairs needed underneath. Converting a tub to a walk-in shower usually runs from about $9,000 to $20,000.

Do I need a permit to remodel a bathroom in Massachusetts?

In most cases, yes. If the work touches plumbing, electrical or framing, Massachusetts generally requires permits, often a building permit plus plumbing and electrical permits. A licensed contractor handles them, and the cost should appear in your estimate.

How long does a bathroom remodel take?

A typical full bathroom remodel takes three to six weeks once work begins, depending on tile work, material lead times and whether plumbing or walls are being moved. Your estimate should include a clear schedule by phase.

How much does a bathroom remodel cost per square foot?

Because bathrooms have many fixed costs in a small space, they cost more per square foot than most rooms. A small hall bath with a mid-range remodel often lands roughly between $450 and $900 per square foot on the North Shore, while larger primary baths often land roughly between $300 and $650. Use these numbers as a sanity check, not as a budget.

Is a condo bathroom remodel different in Massachusetts?

The construction is similar, but condo projects usually require association approval, proof of the contractor's insurance, limited work hours and coordination for shared plumbing risers and elevators. Read your association documents first and share them with your contractor so the estimate reflects the building's rules.
