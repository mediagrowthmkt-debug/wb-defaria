The short answer

What is finish carpentry?

Finish carpentry is the woodwork you see and touch after the walls are closed: casing around windows and doors, baseboard and crown, built-in bookcases and benches, wainscoting and paneling, interior doors and cabinets. It is the last inch of a project, and it is where quality becomes obvious, because every gap, uneven reveal or wavy line is right at eye level.

  • Built-in bookcases, window seats, mudroom benches and closets.
  • Window and door casing, baseboard, crown and chair rail.
  • Wainscoting, board and batten, shiplap and accent walls.
  • Interior doors, barn doors and pocket doors.
  • Kitchen, bathroom and laundry cabinet installation, including vanities.

DeFaria started as a carpentry company, and the company's legal name is still DeFaria Carpentry, Inc. Finish work is where we are most at home.

Built-ins

Built-in bookcases, benches and window seats

Built-ins are the most requested finish carpentry project in winter, and for good reason: they add storage, frame a fireplace or window, and make a room feel designed instead of furnished. Searches for built-in bookcases in Massachusetts peak in January, when families are inside and noticing every bare wall.

  • Bookcases on each side of a fireplace or around a doorway.
  • Window seats with drawers or lift-up storage below.
  • Mudroom benches with cubbies, hooks and shoe storage.
  • Basement shelving, media walls and storage niches under stairs.
  • Home office walls with desk, shelves and closed cabinets.

We build the boxes square, scribe them to walls and floors that are never perfectly flat in older homes, and finish them with face frames, trim and paint so they look original to the house.

Cabinets

Cabinet installation for kitchens, baths and laundry rooms

Good cabinets installed badly look cheap. Cabinet installation is finish carpentry: the boxes have to be level and in one plane even when the floor slopes and the walls bow, so doors line up and countertops can be templated without gaps.

  • Finding the high point of the floor and setting a level line for bases.
  • Shimming and fastening into studs, never just drywall.
  • Scribing fillers and end panels to out-of-plumb walls.
  • Crown, light rail and toe kicks installed tight.
  • Bathroom vanity installation with plumbing coordinated around it.
  • Hardware drilled with a jig so every pull lines up.

Cabinets are a big part of our kitchen remodeling and bathroom remodeling work, and we also install cabinets and vanities homeowners buy on their own.

Trim

Window and door casing, baseboard and crown molding

Trim is what makes a room look finished. Tight miters, even reveals and caulk lines you cannot see are the difference between builder grade and custom. In older homes we also deal with walls that are out of plumb, jambs that are not flush and floors that rise and fall.

  • Casing and wood stools on windows, like the shiplap window in our photo.
  • Door casing with back band or plinth blocks for a classic look.
  • Tall baseboard with a cap and shoe that hides uneven floors.
  • Crown molding coped at inside corners so joints stay closed through the seasons.
  • Chair rail and picture rail in dining rooms and halls.

Massachusetts houses swing from dry winter heat to humid summers, so we cope inside corners and glue and pin outside miters to keep joints from opening.

Wall paneling

Wainscoting, shiplap and accent walls

Paneling adds texture and protects walls in the rooms that take the most wear. It also hides the cracks and patches that older plaster walls collect over the years.

  • Raised or flat panel wainscoting in dining rooms and stair halls.
  • Board and batten in mudrooms, entries and bedrooms.
  • Shiplap walls and ceilings for a farmhouse or coastal look.
  • Beadboard in bathrooms and porches, sealed for humidity.
  • Painted or stained accent walls behind a bed or a TV.

We lay the pattern out from the center of the wall so the ends are balanced, and we plan outlets and switches before the first board goes up.

Doors

Custom doors, barn doors and interior door replacement

Doors are used dozens of times a day, so they have to hang right. We replace hollow-core doors with solid ones, build custom doors and install sliding barn doors where a swinging door does not fit.

  • Pre-hung and slab door replacement, planed and hung to the existing jamb.
  • Sliding barn doors on quality track, sized to cover the opening.
  • Pocket doors for tight halls and bathrooms.
  • Hardware, stops and weatherstrip adjusted so doors close quietly.

The custom barn doors in our photos were built in-house and hung for a recent DeFaria remodel.

Older homes

Matching trim in older Massachusetts homes

Colonials, Victorians and capes across the North Shore were trimmed with profiles you will not find at the lumber yard today. When a remodel removes a wall or adds a window, new trim has to match or the patch shows forever.

  • We pull a sample and build the profile up from stock pieces when possible.
  • When needed, matching profiles can be milled to order.
  • Back band and plinth blocks recreate the depth of older casing.
  • New baseboard is scribed to floors that have settled over a century.

The goal is simple: nobody should be able to tell where the old house ends and the new work begins.

Materials

Paint-grade or stain-grade: choosing the wood

Most built-ins and trim in our area are paint-grade, and the material choice drives both price and durability.

  • Poplar: smooth, stable and the go-to for painted trim and face frames.
  • MDF: very stable for panels and wainscoting, best kept away from wet floors.
  • Maple and birch plywood: strong, flat boxes and shelves that do not sag.
  • Oak, cherry and walnut: stain-grade woods for pieces you want to show the grain.
  • Moisture-resistant products in bathrooms, basements and mudrooms.

We recommend the right mix for each piece instead of one material for everything.

Licensing

Do you need a license for finish carpentry in Massachusetts?

Yes, in most home projects. Work on an existing owner-occupied home of one to four units generally requires a Massachusetts Home Improvement Contractor registration, and anything structural needs a Construction Supervisor License. Registration also gives homeowners access to the state's guaranty fund if something goes wrong.

Before you hire any carpenter, ask for both, plus proof of insurance. DeFaria Construction is licensed and insured, and BBB Accredited with an A+ rating.

Process

How a finish carpentry project runs

  1. Walkthrough, measurements and a conversation about style and use.
  2. Drawings or a clear sketch with dimensions, then a fixed estimate.
  3. Material selection: paint-grade or stain-grade, hardware, finish.
  4. Shop work: boxes, face frames, doors and drawers built and fitted.
  5. Installation in your home, scribed to walls and floors.
  6. Caulk, fill, prime and paint or stain.
  7. Final walkthrough and hardware adjustments.

Most built-ins take one to three weeks from approved design to finished, with only part of that time spent in your home.

Timing

Why winter is the season for built-ins and trim

Finish carpentry is indoor work, so New England winters do not stop it. It is also when families notice the cluttered corners, bare walls and drafty old trim most. Planning built-ins in the fall means they are done before the holidays; planning them in January means they are done well before spring projects take over the calendar.

Many winter projects combine carpentry with a finished basement or a fresh coat from our interior painters.

Mudrooms

Mudroom benches, lockers and entry storage

In a New England house the back door works harder than the front door. Boots, coats, backpacks and dog leashes all land there from November to April. A built-in mudroom bench with cubbies underneath, hooks or open lockers above and a shelf on top turns a cluttered entry into a space that stays organized.

We plan the bench height for sitting and pulling on boots, use a durable painted finish that can be wiped down, and add a boot tray area or a tiled base where wet boots sit. Narrow halls can still fit a shallow version with hooks and a slim shelf.

Fireplace walls

Fireplace surrounds, mantels and built-ins beside the chimney

The recesses on each side of a chimney are a natural place for built-in bookcases or cabinets, and they are one of the most requested projects in older North Shore homes. Lower cabinets hide media equipment and clutter, open shelves above show books and photos, and a mantel ties the wall together.

Woodwork near a fireplace has to respect the clearances required for combustible materials around the firebox. We plan the surround and mantel dimensions with that in mind before anything is built.

Closets and pantries

Closet, pantry and laundry room built-ins

Custom shelving makes small spaces hold far more. A reach-in closet can gain double hanging and drawers, a walk-in can get a full wall of shoe shelves and a bench, and a pantry can go from wire racks to deep wood shelves sized for the things you actually store.

  • Adjustable shelves where needs change over time.
  • Pull-out drawers for heavy items low in a pantry.
  • A folding counter and cabinets above the washer and dryer in a laundry room.

Stairs

Stair treads, risers, balusters and newel posts

A staircase is the most visible piece of finish carpentry in many homes. We replace worn treads and risers, swap carpeted stairs for hardwood treads, install new balusters and newel posts, and repair loose railings that move when you lean on them.

Railing work follows the building code: handrails at a consistent height and graspable, and baluster spacing tight enough that a 4 inch sphere cannot pass through. On an old staircase we match existing profiles or design a simple new style that suits the age of the house.

Ceilings

Coffered ceilings, beams and ceiling details

A plain flat ceiling can become the most memorable part of a room. Coffered ceilings use a grid of beams and trim to add depth to a dining room or study, while box beams in a family room give the look of a timber frame without the structure.

These details are built from the framing up, so we check ceiling height, light locations and any ductwork first. A ceiling detail usually works best in rooms with enough height that the beams do not feel heavy.

Wall trim

Chair rail, picture rail and panel molding

Trim on the walls gives a room proportion. Chair rail protects dining room walls from chair backs and divides two colors, picture rail lets you hang art without nail holes in the plaster, and applied panel molding creates the look of traditional raised panels for a fraction of the cost.

The profiles and heights are chosen to match the rest of the house. In a colonial or Victorian that often means a slightly heavier profile than you would see in a newer home.

Scribing

Fitting woodwork to walls that are not straight

Very few walls in an older Massachusetts house are flat, plumb or square. A built-in set against a wavy plaster wall will show gaps unless it is scribed, which means tracing the shape of the wall onto the edge of the cabinet or panel and cutting it to match.

We build in scribe strips and filler pieces so the finished work sits tight to walls, floors and ceilings, instead of relying on thick caulk lines to hide the gaps. This is a big part of what separates true finish carpentry from installed furniture.

Finishes

Painting, staining and clear coats on built-ins

Painted built-ins are usually finished with a hard enamel that resists fingerprints and wiping. Stain-grade pieces in oak, cherry or walnut get stain matched to existing trim when possible, then a durable clear coat.

Because DeFaria also handles interior painting, the woodwork can be primed, filled and finished by the same team that built it. That keeps the schedule tight and the nail holes, seams and joints invisible in the final finish.

Lighting and outlets

Lighting, outlets and wiring inside built-ins

Built-ins often need power: puck lights or LED strips over shelves, an outlet for a television or charging drawer, or a light inside a pantry. Planning the wiring before the cabinets are built keeps cords hidden and avoids cutting into finished panels later.

Electrical work is done by a licensed electrician with the right permit, coordinated by us so the carpentry and the wiring happen in the right order.

Hardware

Hinges, drawer slides and hardware that last

The parts you touch every day decide how a built-in feels after five years. We use full-extension, soft-close drawer slides, adjustable concealed hinges or traditional butt hinges depending on the style, and solid knobs and pulls sized to the door.

Good hardware costs a little more up front and saves the frustration of sagging doors and drawers that stop closing.

Planning

How we plan a built-in before anything is cut

A custom piece starts with careful measurements of the actual room, including how far the walls lean and where the floor dips. From there we sketch the layout with you, decide what each shelf and drawer will hold, and agree on materials, finish and hardware.

  • Measurements and a simple drawing before the estimate is final.
  • Material and finish samples so there are no surprises.
  • A clear schedule for building, installing and finishing.

Care

Caring for painted and stained woodwork

Woodwork lasts for decades with simple care. Wipe painted surfaces with a soft cloth and mild soap, avoid abrasive cleaners, and keep the leftover paint for touch-ups on chips. For stained wood, dust regularly and use a cleaner made for finished wood.

Keep indoor humidity steady through the winter heating season when you can. Very dry air makes wood shrink and opens small gaps at joints, which usually close again in the summer.

Original woodwork

Repairing and restoring original woodwork

Many North Shore homes still have their original window casings, baseboards and doors. When a piece is damaged, the goal is usually to save it rather than replace a whole room of trim. Cracked casing can be glued and pinned, missing sections of molding can be milled to match, and doors that stick can be planed and rehung.

Saving original work keeps the character of the house and often costs less than tearing everything out. When a piece is beyond repair, we replace only that piece with a matching profile.

Home offices

Built-in desks and home office storage

Working from home has turned spare bedrooms and corners into offices. A built-in desk with drawers, upper shelves and a cabinet for the printer uses every inch of a small room and keeps cables out of sight.

We plan the desk height and depth around the chair and monitor you will actually use, and leave space for wiring and ventilation for electronics inside closed cabinets.

Banquettes

Kitchen banquettes and built-in seating

A banquette along a wall or in a bay window can seat more people than chairs in the same space, and the bench can hide deep drawers or lift-up storage. It works especially well in older kitchens where the table never quite fit.

Seat height, depth and the angle of the back are planned for comfort, and the finish is chosen to handle daily cleaning in a busy kitchen.

Materials

Plywood, poplar or MDF: what built-ins are made of

Each material has a job. Cabinet-grade plywood is strong and holds screws well, so it is used for boxes and shelves that carry weight. Poplar is a stable, smooth hardwood that paints beautifully, which makes it a common choice for face frames and trim. MDF is very flat and takes paint well on large panels, but it does not like moisture, so we keep it away from wet areas.

Your estimate lists the material for each part of the piece, so you know exactly what you are paying for.

Part of a remodel

Finish carpentry as part of a kitchen, bath or basement project

Finish carpentry is often the last step of a bigger project, and it is where a remodel either looks custom or looks rushed. When DeFaria remodels a kitchen, bathroom or basement, the same team plans the trim, built-ins and casing from the start, so the openings, outlets and soffits are framed where the woodwork needs them.

That avoids the common problem of a finished room where the baseboard has to be notched around a vent, a casing runs into a light switch or a new built-in does not quite fit the space left for it. One contractor handling framing, drywall, carpentry and paint means the details are decided once and built in the right order.

If you are only adding trim or a built-in to a finished room, we still look at what is behind the wall first, so outlets, heat registers and blocking for heavy shelves are where they should be. Small checks like these save a lot of patching later.

FAQ

Finish carpentry questions

What does finish carpentry mean?

Finish carpentry is the visible woodwork installed after the walls are closed: trim and casing around doors and windows, baseboard and crown molding, built-in shelves and benches, wainscoting, stairs parts, doors and cabinets. Rough carpentry is the framing you never see; finish carpentry is what you look at every day.

Are finish carpenters expensive?

Finish carpentry is skilled, slow work, so it costs more per hour than general labor, but most projects are smaller than people expect. A pair of built-in bookcases often runs $4,000 to $15,000 and new casing for a door or window about $250 to $600 per opening. You get a fixed, itemized number at the walkthrough.

Do you need a license for finish carpentry in Massachusetts?

Home improvement work on an existing owner-occupied home of one to four units generally requires a Massachusetts Home Improvement Contractor registration, and structural work requires a Construction Supervisor License. Ask any contractor for both before you hire them.

How long does it take to build custom built-ins?

Most built-in bookcases, window seats or mudroom benches take one to three weeks from approved design to installed and painted, depending on size and finish. Much of the work happens in the shop, so the time in your home is shorter.

Can you install cabinets I already bought?

Yes. DeFaria installs kitchen, bathroom and laundry cabinets and vanities supplied by you or ordered through us, including scribing fillers and panels to out-of-plumb walls, setting crown and light rail, and leveling bases so countertops can be templated.

Can you match the trim in my older home?

Usually, yes. Many older Essex and Middlesex County homes have casing and baseboard profiles that are no longer stock. We can build them up from standard pieces or have profiles milled so new work matches the rest of the house.

Is DeFaria Construction licensed and insured?

Yes. DeFaria Construction (DeFaria Carpentry, Inc.) is licensed and insured, BBB Accredited with an A+ rating, and every project is owner-led by Luiz DeFaria. Carpentry is where the company started.

How long does it take to build built-in bookcases?

It depends on size and finish. A pair of painted bookcases beside a fireplace is usually measured, built, installed and finished over a few weeks, with only a few of those days spent working inside your home.

Can finish carpentry be done in a house with uneven walls?

Yes, and that is the normal case in older Massachusetts homes. Built-ins and trim are scribed to the real shape of the walls, floors and ceilings so the finished work fits tight without thick caulk lines.

Do built-ins add value to a home?

Well-built pieces that suit the style of the house, such as fireplace built-ins, mudroom benches and window seats, add everyday storage and are often noticed by buyers. Pieces that are very personal or poorly fitted add less.
