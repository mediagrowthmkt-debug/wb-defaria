Repair or replace

Should you repair the deck or replace it?

Not every old deck needs to come down. If the footings, posts and joists are solid, new decking, railings and stairs can give it years of life for much less than a full rebuild.

Replacement makes sense when the frame shows rot, the ledger is pulling away from the house, the footings have shifted, or the deck simply no longer fits how the family uses the yard. At the walkthrough we check the structure and tell you plainly which path makes sense, with the cost of each.

Planning

Planning a deck that fits the way you live

A deck that looks good on paper can still feel wrong in daily life. Before the estimate we look at:

  • How people move from the kitchen to the grill and to the yard.

Getting these choices right up front is what keeps a deck from feeling too small or awkward a year later.

Care

Keeping your new deck in shape

A little care each year keeps a deck safe and good looking:

  • Sweep leaves and debris so boards dry out after rain.
  • Clear heavy snow with a plastic shovel, never a metal edge.
  • Check railings and stairs each spring for anything loose.
  • Clean and re-seal pressure-treated wood every few years.
  • Wash composite boards with mild soap to keep the surface clean.

We walk you through the care for the material you choose when the job is finished.

Composite or wood

Composite vs. pressure-treated wood decking in New England

Most decks on the North Shore end up as one of two surfaces: pressure-treated pine or a composite board. Both can be built on the same treated frame, so the choice is really about the surface you live on every day.

  • Pressure-treated wood: the lower upfront cost and easy to repair one board at a time. It moves with the seasons, so expect some checking and cupping, and plan to clean and seal it every few years.
  • Composite: a higher upfront cost, no sanding or staining, and color that holds much longer. Boards can get warm in direct July sun, so lighter colors work better on south-facing decks.
  • Hidden fasteners: clips that hold composite or hardwood boards from the side, for a clean surface with no screw heads to catch bare feet.

We bring samples to the walkthrough and price both options side by side, so you can see what the upgrade really costs on your deck instead of guessing from a brochure.

Footings

Deck footings and the Massachusetts frost line

Footings are the part of a deck nobody sees and the part that decides whether it stays level. In eastern Massachusetts, deck footings are typically set about 48 inches deep so they sit below the frost line, and the town building department confirms the exact depth for your address.

A footing that is too shallow lifts when the ground freezes and drops when it thaws. After a few winters that shows up as a sloped floor, gaps at the house and stairs that no longer line up. We size each footing for the load it carries, pour it on undisturbed soil and get it inspected before the frame goes on.

On ledge, sandy soil near the coast or a steep yard, the footing plan changes. That is decided at the walkthrough, not on demolition day.

Ledger and flashing

How a deck should attach to the house

An attached deck hangs on a ledger board bolted to the framing of the house. This connection is where many old decks fail, because water gets behind the ledger and rots the rim of the house.

  • Siding is removed where the ledger goes, so it bears directly on the house framing.
  • Flashing tape and metal flashing keep water from running behind the board.
  • Structural screws or through bolts are spaced to the load, not nailed.
  • Where the house framing cannot carry the load, the deck is built freestanding on its own footings.

Railings and stairs

Railing and stair rules a deck has to meet

Massachusetts follows the state building code, which is based on the International Residential Code. For a typical home deck that means:

  • A guard is required when the walking surface is more than 30 inches above the ground below.
  • Guards on a residential deck are at least 36 inches tall.
  • Balusters are spaced so a 4 inch sphere cannot pass through.
  • Stairs have a maximum riser of 7 3/4 inches and a minimum tread of 10 inches, kept uniform from top to bottom.
  • Stairs with four or more risers get a graspable handrail between 34 and 38 inches high.

Inspectors check these on the final visit. We build to them from the first drawing, so there is nothing to tear out later.

Winter

What a New England winter does to a deck

Snow, ice and freeze-thaw cycles are the hardest test a deck faces here. A deck built for this climate has framing sized for snow load, a slight slope away from the house so meltwater drains, and spacing between boards so water does not sit on the surface.

For homeowners, the simple habits matter most: shovel with a plastic blade, skip rock salt on wood and composite boards and use a product the decking maker approves, and keep downspouts from dumping onto the deck. In spring, look at the stairs and the ledger first, because that is where winter damage shows up.

Porches

Covered porches, screened porches and three-season rooms

Many North Shore homes started with a front porch, and a lot of families want one back. A covered porch needs a roof tied into the house, so it is planned more like a small addition than a deck, with footings, framing, roofing and often electrical for lights and a fan.

A screened porch keeps mosquitoes out in July and still lets the breeze in. A three-season room adds windows so the space works from spring into fall. Each step up adds structure, roofing and permit detail, and we walk you through what changes with each option before anything is priced.

Patios

Paver and stone patios built on a real base

A patio is only as good as what is under it. Pavers and stone need excavation, a compacted gravel base and a slight pitch so water runs away from the foundation. Skipping the base is why some patios sink and heave after one winter.

Patios pair well with a low deck or a set of wide stairs off the kitchen, so the family can move from the house to the yard without a long climb. We plan the deck, the stairs and the patio together so the levels and the drainage work as one.

Zoning and historic districts

Setbacks, zoning and historic district reviews

Beyond the building permit, a deck has to respect the zoning setbacks from property lines, and some lots have easements or wetlands nearby that limit where it can go. Towns such as Salem, Marblehead and Newburyport also have local historic districts where visible exterior changes can need review before work starts.

We check these at the walkthrough, so the deck you approve is one the town can approve too.
