Mid-century homes

Painting a mid-century Burlington ranch, Cape or split-level

Most Burlington homes went up after the 1950s, so a typical repaint is a ranch, Cape or split-level with original plaster or early drywall, open stair landings and builder-grade trim. Split-levels need extra care at the half-flights, where walls are tall and the light changes from level to level.

Paneling and soffits

Painting 1960s paneling, soffits and family rooms in Burlington

Many Burlington family rooms and basements from the 1960s and 1970s still have dark wood or veneer paneling, kitchen soffits over the cabinets and low ceilings with a single light. Painting changes these rooms more than any other single project, but paneling needs the right prep: cleaning, de-glossing, a bonding primer and, if the grooves bother you, filling them before the finish coats.

Soffits and low ceilings look better in a flat, bright white, and the paneling can go a warm white or a soft color that makes the room feel taller. If the paneling is damaged or loose, it is often faster to remove it and hang new drywall, which our carpenters can do in the same project.

Split-levels add one more detail: the half-flight stair walls are tall and visible from two levels, so we plan them as one surface with one color, and set up staging so the walls are reached safely without leaning ladders on fresh paint.

  • Paneling cleaned, de-glossed and primed before paint
  • Grooves filled when you want a smooth wall
  • Soffits and low ceilings in a bright flat white
  • Damaged paneling replaced with drywall when it makes sense

Older homes

Painting older Burlington homes: plaster, lead paint and oil-based trim

Burlington grew fast after the 1950s, so most homes are mid-century Capes, ranches, split-levels and garrison Colonials, with newer Colonials on the edges of town. That brings three things to plan for before painting: plaster that cracks and loosens with age, lead in paint applied before 1978, and trim that was finished with oil paint decades ago.

Loose plaster is re-secured and taped so cracks do not come back through the new paint. In homes built before 1978, sanding or scraping old paint requires lead-safe work practices under federal and Massachusetts rules, with containment and careful cleanup. Latex over old oil trim peels unless the trim is cleaned, scuffed and primed with a bonding primer first.

  • Plaster cracks re-secured and taped before painting.
  • Lead-safe prep in homes built before 1978.
  • Bonding primer over old oil-based trim.
  • Profiles and detail on old trim kept crisp, not buried.

Wallpaper

Wallpaper removal before painting a Burlington room

Old wallpaper is one of the most common surprises in Burlington bedrooms, baths and dining rooms. Removing it properly takes longer than painting, so it is priced as its own line in the estimate.

After removal, any remaining adhesive is washed off, because paint over paste can crack and peel. Torn drywall paper is sealed and skimmed, and plaster is patched where the old paper pulled it loose.

  • Paper scored, steamed or soaked and scraped.
  • Old paste washed off the wall.
  • Torn drywall face sealed and skim coated.
  • Primer chosen for walls that held wallpaper.

Kitchens and baths

Painting kitchens and bathrooms in Burlington homes

Kitchens and bathrooms are the hardest rooms on paint. Steam, grease and constant cleaning wear a flat or matte finish out quickly, so these rooms get a satin or semi-gloss paint built for scrubbing and humidity.

Kitchen walls are degreased before painting so the new coat bonds, and bathroom ceilings with mildew are cleaned and treated before primer. We also check that the bath fan actually vents outside, because no paint can fix a room that never dries out.

  • Satin or semi-gloss, moisture-resistant finishes.
  • Kitchen walls degreased before primer.
  • Mildew cleaned and treated on bath ceilings.
  • Exhaust fan checked so the room can dry.

Basements

Painting a finished basement or lower level in Burlington

Lower levels in Burlington homes are often darker and cooler than the rest of the house, so color and light reflectance matter even more. Light, warm colors with a higher light reflectance value make a basement family room feel bigger.

Drywall in a finished basement is painted like any other room. Bare masonry is different: it needs to be dry, clean and free of efflorescence, and it takes a masonry primer or coating made for concrete. If the wall is damp, the moisture is solved first.

Wall repair

Drywall and plaster repair: patch, skim coat or replace?

A Burlington painting estimate always says how each wall will be repaired, because the repair decides how the finished room looks. Hairline cracks get taped and filled; a wall covered in old patches gets a skim coat; plaster that sags or sounds hollow is re-secured before anything else.

We would rather spend an extra day on a skim coat than paint over a wall that will crack again in the first winter.

  • Hairline cracks taped and filled.
  • Skim coat on walls with many patches or torn paper.
  • Loose plaster re-secured to the lath.
  • Damaged sections replaced and feathered in.

Application

Brush, roller or spray: how the paint goes on

Most interior walls and ceilings are cut in by brush at the edges and rolled in the field, which gives an even texture and lets the crew work while the family is home. Spraying is faster on empty rooms, new construction and cabinet doors, but it needs full masking and time to set up.

Trim and doors are usually brushed with a leveling enamel that flows out and hides brush marks. Cabinet doors are often sprayed off site or in a protected area for a smooth factory-like finish.

  • Cut in by brush, rolled in the field on walls.
  • Leveling enamel brushed on trim and doors.
  • Spray for cabinets and empty rooms.
  • Two finish coats over a primed surface.

Trim paint

Oil, latex or waterborne enamel for trim and doors

Trim and doors take the most wear in a Burlington house, so the paint has to be hard and washable. We check whether the existing paint is oil or latex, prime with a bonding primer where needed, and finish with a durable enamel.

Waterborne enamels dry harder than standard wall paint, level out to hide brush strokes and do not yellow the way old oil paint does over time.
